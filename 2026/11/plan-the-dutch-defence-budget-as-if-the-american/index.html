Plan the Dutch defence budget as if the American cheque never arrives

Congress authorises the money, the president decides whether it moves, and this year showed which half wins. The cabinet should stop writing American funds and troops into our planning as if they were certain.

7 November 2026

Plan the Dutch defence budget as if the American cheque never arrives. That is the conclusion the cabinet must draw from the United States midterm elections, in which voters on 3 November 2026 handed Democrats control of both the House of Representatives and the Senate, and from what Washington did long before the vote. European defence stocks fell on the result, with Rheinmetall, a German arms maker, down 3.2 percent and BAE Systems, a British arms maker, down 5.2 percent by mid-morning, but the market worry is the small part. The real problem sits in our own budget papers, which still treat American money and American soldiers as if they were booked.

Take the money first. Earlier in 2026 the Pentagon delayed 400 million dollars in Ukraine Security Assistance Initiative military assistance for months, money Congress had already authorised. On 16 October 2026 President Trump issued a broad waiver of the sanctions under the Lindsey Graham Act, which the Senate passed with 86 votes in August, days after the Treasury lifted restrictions on Russian diesel purchases. Authorised by Congress is not the same as spent by the executive, and the record of this year shows which half wins. Nevada Joan Lee, a policy fellow at the European Council on Foreign Relations, advised Europeans to treat American earmarked funds as intent, not commitment. We should not treat it as anything more than that: if we book our Ukraine support against that intent, we build a budget line on intent, and that is not a budget line.

Mike Levin, a Democratic representative for California's 49th district, says his party will use every legislative and oversight tool available to ensure Ukraine receives the security assistance it needs. A Democratic Congress can write Ukraine money into law, but the president might not spend it, which is the same gap the Nevada Joan Lee point names. Then the soldiers. Congress has set a statutory floor on American troop numbers in Europe, and the president contests that floor, while the Pentagon's force posture review, expected by December 2026, is likely to shift conventional military responsibility in NATO towards European ownership to free resources for the Indo-Pacific. Undersecretary of war Elbridge Colby calls the shift NATO 3.0. Dutch planning for deterrence should not assume the floor holds.

Democrats, it is said, will fix this. Levin's pledge is sincere, and it is not enough. James M. Lindsay of the Council on Foreign Relations made the checkable point: a Democratic House may bristle at President Trump's diplomacy with Russia, but it cannot compel him to change it, and most lawmakers hesitate to oppose a president of their own party. A Congress writes the money; the president decides whether it moves. That is the intent, not commitment, point again, and it stands. The 400 million dollars was delayed for months earlier in 2026, before the midterms, despite congressional authorisation.

The demand lands in The Hague, not Washington. When the cabinet sends the 2027 national budget to the House of Representatives (Tweede Kamer), the chamber should require the government to publish its defence and Ukraine-support figures twice: once counting the American funds Congress authorised and the American troops Washington has pledged to Europe, once counting neither. The gap between those two columns is the risk we now carry unpriced, and the Dutch treasury should cover it openly, the way we cover any other commitment we have made. Hope is the one thing the treasury cannot book. Our own soldiers, ships and euros are.
